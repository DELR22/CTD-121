Student Learning Outcomes

This section is for reference. It is not part of the game. Read it to understand what the Final Project is designed to teach you.

Bloom’s Taxonomy (2001)

  1. Remember the graphic conventions of architectural drawing, and apply them consistently across multiple floor plan iterations.
  2. Understand the relationship between user behavior, spatial need, and built form by analyzing client interviews and translating observations into a written spatial profile.
  3. Apply design process tools in sequence to generate a spatially logical residential design.
  4. Analyze the functional and social dynamics of a shared residence by evaluating how spatial organization supports the specific needs of its inhabitants.
  5. Evaluate design decisions across iterative drafts by interpreting instructor feedback, identifying design problems, and producing documented corrections in subsequent phases.
  6. Create a fully furnished, multi-scale floor plan and a freestanding foam-board presentation that communicates the complete arc of the design process, from user research to a resolved spatial layout.

CIDA / NASAD Standards

  • Design Process: Demonstrate the ability to apply a sequential, iterative design process, making decisions that are traceable back to user-specific evidence.
  • Global Context for Design: Design for real individuals whose cultural habits, social patterns, and personal preferences shape spatial requirements, developing sensitivity to the specificity of human experience as a design driver.
  • Human-Centered Design: Conduct original user research through direct conversation, synthesize findings, and use that knowledge as the functional basis for all subsequent spatial design decisions.
  • Interior Building Systems & Integration: Apply scale, proportion, wall thickness, door clearances, and circulation logic to produce a technically compliant schematic floor plan at different scales.
  • Design Communication: Communicate design intent through partial and final technical documents, reference boards, and a physical presentation structure that fully narrates the design process.
  • Collaboration: Engage real clients in a participatory validation process, modeling professional client-designer communication.

Section 01

Introduction & Scenario

Interior design is, at its core, a discipline of translation. A designer receives raw human experience and translates it into built form.

This final project tracks the design process. Students will design for people they know. This proximity lets them shape the project to their peculiarities and rhythms, not to generic assumptions about how people live.

The Scenario

You are an interior designer who has recently won a small lottery. With your winnings, you decide to purchase a modest house and transform it into something far more meaningful than a private residence: a shared home, a hub of friendship, creativity, and community, where two of your best friends will live together.

The design challenge is to thoughtfully design this shared residence so it functions not only as a place to live, but as a space that actively supports and nurtures the friendship among its inhabitants.

The Iterative Process

The design process is iterative, not linear. You will receive feedback on your documents as Red Marks. Leave those markings as evidence of the process. DO NOT ERASE OR COVER THEM. Make corrections in the subsequent phase. At the end, your final deliverable will reveal the full story of your design development, including Red Marks. This project is not only about the result, but about the value of the process that supports design decisions.

Client Empathy

The two friends are the primary clients and the most important research sources. Every spatial decision in the design must be traceable back to something learned from conversations with them.

Ask about daily routines, sleeping habits, cooking preferences, work or study needs, social patterns, noise sensitivity, and personal style. Ask what they love and hate about where they currently live. Ask about the DOs and DON’Ts you should consider.

Section 02

Client Interaction, Research & Project Conceptualization

2A

Client Interaction & Research

Use Format 1

  1. Download and print Format 1 from: alabama.box.com/s/2gk0xdxmq80pafy4ixm4nhekz3ez2wlh
  2. Conduct a minimum of five text message conversations with each of the two friends.
    • Explore lifestyle and daily habits, spatial preferences, current living frustrations, and how the three of you envision your shared life together.
  3. Take screenshots of these conversations and print them.
  4. Paste the printed screenshots on Format 1 where indicated.
  5. Write down their preferences, needs, and desires.
  6. Translate client preferences, needs, and desires into terms of what the project must do (DOs) and what it must avoid (DON’Ts).
  7. Write down the preferences, needs, desires, DOs, and DON’Ts as indicated in Format 1.
  8. Write down the conclusion of the research and analysis as indicated in Format 1.

IMPORTANT NOTE: All written texts must be done using technical lettering.

2B

Project Conceptualization

Use Format 2

  1. Download and print Format 2 from: alabama.box.com/s/syaw6da9f5duoonrg8chbhsrgw6cqgru
  2. Draw the Bubble Diagram as a conceptual scheme, with circles that represent each space’s relative importance in size and role within the project.
    • Connect bubbles with the appropriate line-type symbols. For a review of these conventions, refer to Assignment 06.
    • Develop the project’s Adjacency Matrix using the three-symbol key for all spaces in the program.
    ●Primary Adjacency—must be next to each other.
    ○Secondary Adjacency—should be close or convenient.
    –No Relation—proximity does not matter.
  3. Create and write down the project’s Design Concept Statement based on client research, analysis, and reflection about their needs, preferences, and desires, as well as the project’s governing DOs and DON’Ts.
  4. Use the DREAMS-4-Mood Environments App as instructed in class to create an AI-generated Project Mood Board.
    • Take screenshots of the process and paste each screenshot, as well as the AI-generated mood board, as indicated in the format.
  5. Based on the graphic references provided by the AI-generated board, compose a physical mood board using magazine clippings and material samples from the material library in the basement of Doster Hall.
  6. Create your own mood board as indicated in Format 2.

IMPORTANT NOTE: All written text must be done using technical lettering.

Section 06

Foam Board Presentation: The Synthesis Object

06

The Synthesis Object

Five 11×17″ rigid foam boards

Objective: Create a narrative within a volumetric composition that reconstructs and explains the design process.

  1. Mount all work on five rigid foam boards measuring 11×17 inches, using the completed work from the previous phases.
  2. Connect the boards, creating a narrative object.

Structure Requirements

Stand independently: the structure may not lean against a wall or another object.

Read in sequence: panels must be arranged so a viewer can follow the design process from most abstract to most concrete.

The Synthesis Object is not decoration. It is an argument: the panel sequence should tell the story of how a designer moves from client research, schematic drawings, and data to a fully realized floor plan.

    The password · Who was she?

    Zaha Hadid

    Iraqi-British architect and designer · Baghdad, 1950 – Miami, 2016

    Dame Zaha Hadid studied mathematics at the American University of Beirut and architecture at the Architectural Association in London, where she graduated in 1977. After working with Rem Koolhaas and Elia Zenghelis at the Office for Metropolitan Architecture, she founded her own practice, Zaha Hadid Architects, in London in 1979.

    Her first major built work was the Vitra Fire Station in Germany (1993). It was followed by projects such as the MAXXI museum in Rome, the London Aquatics Centre for the 2012 Olympic Games, and the Heydar Aliyev Center in Baku, known for their fluid, sweeping forms. In 2004 she became the first woman to receive the Pritzker Architecture Prize, and in 2016 she was the first woman awarded the Royal Gold Medal of the Royal Institute of British Architects in her own right. She also designed furniture, interiors, and objects.
